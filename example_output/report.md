# Oat Milk vs. Almond Milk: Which Is Better for the Environment?

Both oat and almond milk are far more environmentally friendly than dairy — plant-based milks generally produce roughly one-third (or less) of the greenhouse gas emissions and require a fraction of the land that dairy production does. But when the two are compared directly against each other, the picture is more nuanced: **oat milk wins decisively on water use**, while **almond milk has a slight edge on land use and, in some analyses, a marginally smaller carbon footprint per liter.** Overall, most environmental researchers and outlets consider oat milk the more sustainable *all-around* choice, mainly because of the water issue.

## Carbon Emissions
On a strict greenhouse-gas basis, the two are close, and some datasets (based on Poore & Nemecek's widely cited 2018 *Science* study) even give almond milk a slight edge: roughly **0.7 kg CO2e per liter for almond milk vs. about 0.9 kg CO2e per liter for oat milk**. The difference comes partly from oat farming's heavier use of nitrogen fertilizer, which releases nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. However, both are drastically lower-carbon than dairy milk, which produces roughly three times the emissions of any plant-based alternative.

## Water Use
This is where the two diverge the most sharply. Oat milk requires only about **48 liters of water per liter produced**, since oats are largely rain-fed and grown in cooler, wetter climates such as the northern US, Canada, and Europe. Almond milk, by contrast, requires roughly **370+ liters of water per liter produced** (about 74 liters for a single glass) — a footprint comparable to, or in water-stressed regions even worse than, dairy milk. Over 80% of the world's almonds are grown in California, a state that faces recurring severe drought, meaning almond cultivation places significant pressure on already-strained aquifers and rivers. As the World Resources Institute notes, the real-world impact of that water use depends heavily on *where* it's drawn — water used in an arid, water-scarce region carries a much heavier ecological cost than the same volume used somewhere water-rich.

## Land Use and Biodiversity
Land-use figures are fairly close, with almond trees sometimes requiring slightly less land per liter of milk than oats (around 0.5 m² vs. 0.8 m²), and almond orchards can even act as long-term carbon sinks over the trees' lifespan. However, almond monocultures raise separate biodiversity concerns: California's almond industry depends on trucking in an estimated two-thirds of all commercial honeybee colonies in the US each spring for pollination. This mass, high-stress relocation of bees has been linked to elevated colony collapse rates, disease spread, and pesticide exposure (including glyphosate), making almond farming a notable driver of pollinator stress. Oats, as an annual crop, can be rotated with other crops to help maintain soil health, which is a modest additional sustainability advantage.

## Protein and Practical Considerations
Neither milk is particularly high in protein compared to dairy, soy, or pea milk, so this isn't a major differentiator between them. Both can also be made at home (blending oats or almonds with water and straining) to reduce packaging and processing emissions, which is a low-effort way to shrink either option's footprint further.

## Conclusion
There is no absolute winner on every single metric — almond milk may produce marginally less carbon per liter in some models and needs a bit less land, but it comes with a substantial water cost concentrated in a drought-prone region, plus documented stress on bee populations from mass pollination practices. Oat milk uses dramatically less water (as little as one-eighth of almond milk's footprint) and can be grown with fewer geographic constraints, at the cost of somewhat higher fertilizer-related emissions and land use. On balance, **oat milk is generally regarded as the more environmentally sustainable choice, particularly in water-stressed regions**, while almond milk remains a reasonable option primarily where water is abundant and carbon emissions are the main concern. Either way, both are meaningfully better for the planet than conventional dairy milk.
